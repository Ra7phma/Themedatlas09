Medical school requirements

A short introduction to each school's teaching style, interview format and how each university uses the UCAT, the Situational Judgement Test (SJT), GCSEs and A-levels, plus the lowest UCAT score invited to interview last year (2026 entry) or the published minimum where that is all a school shares. Every row also carries a predicted line for 2027 entry. All 51 UK medical schools that take students for 2027 entry are listed.

New UCAT format. Since 2025 the UCAT has three scored subtests (Verbal Reasoning, Decision Making, Quantitative Reasoning), each 300 to 900, so the total is out of 2700. Abstract Reasoning has been removed. The SJT is reported separately as Band 1 to 4. Scores from before 2025 were out of 3600 and can't be compared directly, so every figure here is on the 2700 scale.
Learning styles explained. Integrated: science and clinical medicine are taught together from early on, usually with patient contact in the first year. PBL / case-based: you learn mainly in small groups, working through patient cases and finding out what you need to know. Traditional (science-first): a few years of mostly science-based study before clinical training. These are broad labels, and most schools blend styles. Summaries are brief and courses change, so confirm on each university's course page and at open days.
Please check before you apply. Universities set interview thresholds after applications close, and they change every year. A "lowest invited" score is what the last cohort needed, not a guarantee. A "published minimum" is a cut-off the university states in advance, which is not the same thing as the lowest score actually invited. Where a school uses a combined points score or publishes no figure, the table says so. Always confirm on each university's own admissions page before choosing your four medicine choices on UCAS.

Schools without a figure to rank

These schools either don't use the UCAT, set no threshold, or publish too little on the 2700 scale to place on a chart. Previous versions of this page gave them invented numbers; they are listed here instead.

Chart scale runs from 1500 to 2400; anything outside it sits at the edge. "Published minimum" means the school states the floor in advance, so clearing it is the start of the process rather than a place on the interview list. "Composite" means the UCAT is only one part of a points score, usually alongside GCSEs or predicted grades, so the bar is a rough guide to where that points line landed, not a UCAT threshold.

How the labels work: "At or above" means your total matches the predicted line. "Within 100 below" means you are close enough that strong GCSEs or a good SJT band could still matter, at schools that score those things. "More than 100 below" means the predicted line is out of reach on the UCAT alone.

UCAT is only one part of your application. Many universities also weigh GCSEs, the SJT band, predicted grades, the three personal statement questions and the interview.

UCAT resources

Where to practise, learn the test and find advice. Start with the official free material, then add one good question bank if you need more.

Prices, free trials and what's included change often, so check each site before you pay. You don't need to buy every resource, and paid courses are never required to get a good score.

UCAT preparation tips

  • Start with the official site. The real practice tests use the same interface as test day, including the on-screen calculator, so get comfortable with them early.
  • Learn first, then time yourself. Work through each section untimed to understand the question types, then move to timed practice as the test gets closer.
  • Keep an error log. Write down why you got each question wrong (misread, ran out of time, didn't know the method). Patterns show you what to fix.
  • Build a steady routine. Many students prepare for roughly six to eight weeks, a little each day. Start earlier if you have exams or a busy schedule. The revision tracker below can help you stick to it.
  • Don't forget the SJT. It is reported as a band, and many universities reject Band 4. Read the official guidance and the GMC's professional standards to understand what good professional behaviour looks like.
  • Check bursaries and deadlines. Eligible UK students may get help with the test fee. Registration and booking windows are on the official UCAT website.
  • One good question bank is enough. Doing fewer questions and reviewing them properly beats rushing through several banks.

MMI and interview practice

Most medical schools use interviews to decide who gets an offer. Many use Multiple Mini Interviews (MMIs): a series of short stations, each with its own task and scorer. Others use panel interviews or group tasks, so check the interview tag next to each university above.

MMI basics

  • Each station is a fresh start. If one goes badly, reset and focus on the next.
  • Take a breath before you answer. A short pause to structure your thoughts looks confident.
  • Be yourself. Interviewers can spot memorised scripts, so practise ideas and structure, not word-for-word answers.
  • Practise out loud. Use the tool below with a friend, a teacher or even a mirror, and try recording yourself.
  • Read around the topics. Follow NHS and healthcare news, and read the GMC's professional standards and the NHS values.

MMI and interview resources

Work experience and volunteering

Medical schools want to see that you understand what a career in healthcare involves and that you have shown care for others. Formal shadowing isn't required, and volunteering and caring roles count just as much.

Eligibility, minimum ages and availability change often. Check each provider's website before applying. Never pay anyone to guarantee you a placement at an NHS site, and remember that paid programmes are not required or favoured by medical schools.

Extracurriculars for a well-rounded application

Medical schools aren't looking for a long list of random hobbies. They want evidence of key skills: communication, empathy, leadership, resilience, teamwork and self-care. Use your personal statement and interviews to reflect on what you have learned from what you do outside of lessons.

Music, arts and sports

Playing in an orchestra, competing in a team sport or completing a marathon demonstrates discipline, commitment, time management and the ability to handle pressure. It also gives you vital outlets for stress during demanding medical school terms.

Part-time jobs

Working in retail, hospitality or tutoring teaches you how to communicate with people from all walks of life, manage difficult situations, take responsibility and handle customer service under pressure—skills that are directly applicable to talking with patients and working in healthcare teams.

Leadership and mentoring

Being a prefect, running a school club, mentoring younger students or captaining a team builds leadership, organization and empathy. Reflecting on how you motivate others or handle team conflict is fantastic material for interviews.

Wide reading and science interests

Reading around medicine (such as books by Henry Marsh, Atul Gawande or Kayt Sukumar) or keeping up with healthcare news in the BMJ or Guardian helps you form a realistic view of the profession's challenges, ethics and rewards.
